Designed by one hand, woven by another: "Woven Monuments", Senegalese tapestries at the Baltimore Museum of Art (fine art / art history)

"Monumental" is usually a word for painting and sculpture; Senegal's post-independence state gave it to the loom.

T8 Asia and postcolonial frame · Textile structure

Why it matters

"Woven Monuments: Tapestry and Identity in Senegal" runs 25 Oct 2026 to 7 Mar 2027 at the Baltimore Museum of Art. The museum calls it the first U.S. museum exhibition in 45 years dedicated to Senegalese tapestries: about 12 large works made mostly at the state-supported Manufactures sénégalaises des arts décoratifs de Thiès, by artists including Papa Ibra Tall, Théodore Diouf and Bocar Pathé Diong, blending modernist abstraction with Senegalese history, daily life and landscape (BMA page). A secondary report says one work credits the designer and the weaver separately (Evolving Humanities, 2026). Lineage: the Thiès manufactory dates to the 1960s, after independence in 1960. For a textile designer this is a clean study in construction: an image drawn first, then translated yarn by yarn, with flat color areas and bold outline lines that survive weaving. It also sets a useful question for the portfolio: where does authorship sit between artwork, cartoon and loom?

The idea

Who made this: the person who drew it, or the person who wove it?

The Baltimore show puts Senegal's tapestries at the scale of monuments and names more than one maker behind each. Read as construction, the image has to be broken into a limited set of color areas and outlines the loom can hold. Pair a posterized cartoon with the finished weave and the translation becomes visible. Designer-to-designer: the line weight is the yarn weight.
