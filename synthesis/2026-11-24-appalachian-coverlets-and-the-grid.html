Bedspreads before op art: Appalachian coverlets at the Asheville Art Museum, "Warp, Weft, and the Grid" (art history / textile)

"Overshot weaving did the grid first, on a four-shaft loom." Nineteenth-century coverlets hang beside Anni Albers and Julian Stanczak and look like the same language.

T9 Wear, fragility and the trace of time · Textile structure

Why it matters

The Asheville Art Museum (North Carolina) shows "Warp, Weft, and the Grid" from 16 or 17 Jul 2026 to 17 Jan 2027 (the museum page says 17 Jul; Mountain Xpress says opening 16 Jul with a members' preview). It sets 19th and early 20th century southern Appalachian overshot coverlets next to works by Josef and Anni Albers, Julian Stanczak and early computer-based art. The curators say it does not claim direct influence on op art; it points to a shared visual language and to women's labor outside the art-world frame (Mountain Xpress, 13 Aug 2026). Contemporary weaver Kimberly English contributes "By and By", a doublecloth weaving built on overshot logic (same article). Overshot is a clean construction lesson: a plain-weave ground with a pattern weft floating on top, so complex motifs come from a simple loom. That is the link to your own structure work, and to modern woven upholstery, where float and ground decide hand and wear. Note: this is not a last-48-hours story; it is a current show, found while widening the search, and is included as art history with a recent press piece.

The idea

The op art grid had a prequel, and it was woven on a home loom.

Overshot lets a simple loom produce dizzying geometry, which is exactly why these coverlets sit so comfortably beside Albers. Then ask where in car or furniture upholstery that same float-over-ground logic could earn a pattern.
