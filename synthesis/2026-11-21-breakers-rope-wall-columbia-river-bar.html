Waves tied in rope: Margie McDonald, "Breakers" at the Columbia River Maritime Museum (fiber art / textile structure)

A 25-foot wall of knotted and unlaid rope turns the standing waves of the Columbia River Bar into relief you can read as construction.

T6 Place and nature · Textile structure

Why it matters

Port Townsend Shipwrights (post dated 19 Nov 2026, the company's own blog, so a partial source) says "Breakers" by fiber artist and former yacht rigger Margie McDonald opens to the public in Mariners Hall, Columbia River Maritime Museum, Astoria, Oregon, on 20 Nov 2026, and was the museum's first commissioned artwork. It is five panels of 5 by 5 feet, about 200 pounds each, from more than two miles of rope; thousands of overhand knots build the waves up to about 8 inches; a couple thousand feet of three-strand rope were unlaid for the soft background; double braid gives the tighter, smoother areas; silvery Dyneema offcuts from the sail loft run through the background; hundreds of crystals were sewn on; about 600 hours in total. It is a clean lesson in construction before color: the same sepia rope reads as water because of structure alone (twist, braid, knot depth, unlaying). It also shows how rigging skills (splicing, knotting) are textile skills. The three-strand versus double-braid contrast is a usable vocabulary for soft-surface relief in interiors; feasibility, abrasion and cleanability are Engineering and Lab questions.

The idea

Same color, same rope: only the structure turns it into water.

The pairing is rope as relief, built from three constructions: twist, braid and knot. Designer to designer: it shows how much a surface can say with one color if you control height and density, and it brings sail-loft scrap into the museum.
