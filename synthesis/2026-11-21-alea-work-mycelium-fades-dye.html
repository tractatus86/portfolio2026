Fungus as a bleach: Aléa Work lets mycelium fade dyed fabric (material design / process)

A forgotten fungal culture removed the color from dyed cloth, and the Paris studio Aléa Work now treats that fading as a pattern-making method.

T9 Wear, fragility and the trace of time · Material innovation

Why it matters

A feature dated 19 Nov 2026 reports that Aléa Work, founded in 2021 by Miriam Josi and Stella Lee Prowse, found that fungi left on dyed fabric past the planned end had decolorized it in irregular pale patterns. The project is called Mycelial Patterns and studies how fungi break down dyes. The studio also grows chairs from mycelium and from discarded materials (Back to Dirt, Dirty Chairs). The designers say bio-based is not automatically sustainable and that these materials still need extensive testing. For color and finish work this reverses the usual question: instead of fixing color against wear, the wear is the surface. It links to dye fastness, fading and patina, which any soft-material designer deals with, and shows decay as a design stage rather than failure. A controlled fade map could be a visual language for "worn" without distress printing. Feasibility for interiors (hygiene, durability, lightfastness, odor) is an Engineering and Lab question and unknown.

The idea

What if the fade on a fabric was made by a fungus, on purpose?

The pairing is dyed cloth with the pattern its decay leaves. Designer to designer: it asks whether a worn look can be grown and controlled instead of printed, and what it takes to stop it when you want it to stop.
